## A note on privacy

The playbook says **HHCS will protect data privacy and the security of all confidential student and staff information, and comply with FERPA and COPPA.** Starter Hermes tries to live up to that:

- **Memory is off.** Nothing you say is remembered between chats.
- **No personal data is collected** by the profile itself.
- **No secret keys are stored** in the profile. You sign in with your provider; that's their job to keep safe.
- **Your chats live on your own machine.** They aren't sent anywhere besides the model you choose.

If a teacher or parent asks "is this safe?" — the honest answer is: **safer than a browser tab, not a substitute for adult supervision.** Keep doing the things the playbook says: talk before you act, disclose when you use AI, and verify what AI tells you.

## 1. Who we are and why this matters

**AI is already part of your life.** Maybe you use it to check a homework problem, listen to a song it picked for you, or talk to a chatbot when you have a question. It's already there.

**Huber Heights City Schools is choosing AI on purpose, with your best interests at the center.** Three reasons:

1. **Future readiness.** Knowing how AI works is becoming as basic as reading and math.
2. **Empowering educators.** When teachers spend less time on routine tasks, they spend more time with you.
3. **Personalized learning.** AI can help break down hard ideas, offer practice paths, and be available 24/7.

We are not adopting AI because it is shiny. We are not ignoring the real concerns families have raised. **This guide is how we navigate AI openly together.** It protects critical thinking, data privacy, and the teacher-student relationships that make HHCS special.

## 2. Our vision for AI at HHCS

**We use AI to enhance learning. It does not replace learning.**

Three pillars guide us. You will see them in every section of this guide.

| Pillar | What it means for you |
| --- | --- |
| **Empower Educators** | Teachers use AI to handle the routine work so they can spend more time doing what only a person can do — teaching you. |
| **Enhance Learning** | You can use AI as a learning assistant and tutor. It can break down complex problems, offer personalized pathways, and be available 24/7. |
| **Protect Integrity** | We value **how you learn** (the process) as much as the final answer (the product). AI should support the process. |

## 3. Shared language: our AI glossary

**Shared language is shared power.** These words are used in the playbook and in this guide. Learn them and you've already done a lot.

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** | Technology that does tasks that usually need human intelligence — recognizing patterns, solving problems, or creating content. |
| **Generative AI** | AI that creates new content (text, images, etc.) based on patterns from existing data. ChatGPT, Gemini, MagicSchool, and Starter Hermes are examples. |
| **AI Tool** | An AI app or system that generates outputs based on human inputs. |
| **Prompt** | The specific question or instruction you give an AI tool. Better prompts = better results. |
| **Hallucination** | When AI states something confidently that is actually wrong or made up. **Always verify AI output before using it.** |
| **AI Disclosure / Citation** | Naming the AI tool you used and describing what it helped you do. It's like citing a book or a website. |
| **Academic Integrity** | Using AI to support your learning, not to replace your thinking. The final work must reflect your own effort and voice. |
| **Human-in-the-Loop** | A person (you or your teacher) must always review, verify, and take responsibility for any AI content. |
| **Process vs. Product** | We value **how you learn and think** (the process) as much as the final essay or answer (the product). |

## 4. The Red / Yellow / Green framework

**Assignments are color-coded to support learning options.** Ask your teacher if you are unsure what color an assignment is.

This is not about fear. It's about clarity. When everyone knows the rules, AI becomes a tool rather than a source of anxiety.

### 🟢 GREEN — Open Use

Your teacher approves you to use AI to brainstorm, learn, or draft. **You must be transparent about how AI was used.**

- **You can:** brainstorm ideas, use a tutor bot to break down a math problem, create study flashcards, generate a first draft to then rewrite in your own voice.
- **You must submit:** your original prompt + the AI output + your final edited work. Name the tool you used (e.g., `starter-hermes`, ChatGPT, MagicSchool, Canva AI).

### 🟡 YELLOW — Limited Use (Disclosure Required)

Your teacher explicitly directs which parts of an assignment AI can be used for. **You must be transparent about how AI was used.**

- **Examples:** refining an existing draft, summarizing a long article, checking code logic, getting feedback on a paragraph's structure.
- **You must submit:** your original prompt + the AI output + your final edited work. Name the tool you used.

### 🔴 RED — Human Only (No AI Permitted)

**No AI tools permitted.** The task is assessing your thinking, your foundational skills, or your authentic personal voice.

Per Policy EDEC, submitting AI-generated content as your own original work is always a violation.

- **Examples:** high-stakes assessments, personal narratives, foundational skills (phonics, basic math facts), in-class timed writing.

### Grade-band guidance

| Grade band | What this looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **K–5 (Elementary)** | Mostly **RED** assignments. The focus is on building foundational skills — reading, writing, and math — without digital shortcuts. Any AI use is teacher-led and exploratory only. |
| **6–12 (Secondary)** | Shifts toward **YELLOW** and **GREEN** as you learn to use AI as a research and learning assistant. Disclosure requirements apply. Teachers explicitly label assignments when AI use is allowed. |

## 5. How to disclose AI use (yellow and green assignments)

**When your teacher permits AI use, you must provide a brief AI Disclosure Note.** It's like citing a source.

| Step | What to include |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Name the tool** | Which AI did you use? (e.g., `starter-hermes`, ChatGPT, MagicSchool, Canva AI, Google Gemini) |
| **2. Describe the use** | What did you ask it to do? (e.g., *"I used AI to brainstorm five essay topics"* or *"AI explained the quadratic formula steps"*) |
| **3. Show verification** | How did you check the AI's output for errors or hallucinations? What did you change or add from the AI's response? |

> **A starter-hermes example.** If you used Starter Hermes to explain a black hole, your disclosure might look like:
>
> *"I used starter-hermes (a chat assistant) to ask 'Explain black holes without assuming I know physics.' I then re-read the response and compared it to two articles from NASA's website. I added my own example about a beach ball and a bowling ball to make it easier for my class to picture."*

## 6. On AI detection tools

**AI detectors are not 100% accurate.** They can flag innocent students. We do not rely on detector scores alone.

If a teacher has concerns, **the first step is always a conversation, never an automatic penalty.** Our goal is to understand your thinking, not punish uncertainty.

## 7. Our shared commitments

**Everyone in our community has a role to play.** Here is what we each commit to.

### Students

- **Learning takes effort.** Use AI when approved, not to skip the thinking.
- **Disclose AI use** and follow citation expectations when required.
- **Verify AI output** — it can "hallucinate" wrong or made-up information.
- **Develop your critical thinking and voice.**
- Use AI on yellow and green assignments **as directed by teachers.**

### Educators and staff

- **Clearly label assignments** as red, yellow, or green so you know what is expected.
- Use AI to reduce administrative tasks, then **reinvest that time with students.**
- Design learning that **protects critical thinking and develops student voice.**
- Treat first AI-misuse incidents as **teaching moments**, not just punishments.
- **Never input confidential student data** into any AI tool.

### Families and community

- **Ask your child whether AI is helping them learn, or just giving answers.**
- Encourage critical thinking about online information; AI can be wrong.
- **Monitor privacy settings** on AI tools used outside school hours.
- Reach out to teachers with any questions about AI use on homework.
- **Partner with us:** attend parent listening sessions and share your perspective.

### District leadership

- **Vet AI tools** for data privacy and security before district-wide use.
- Provide **ongoing professional development** so educators feel confident and supported.
- **Ensure equitable access** — AI should not widen the digital divide.
- Publish a public **"Approved Tool List"** on the HHCS website.
- **Review this playbook at least once a year.**

## 8. Response protocol: conversation before consequences

**Gray areas will arise. Our first move is always a conversation.**

We are all on the forefront of AI in teaching and learning. Because AI is new and evolving, misunderstandings will happen. We will grow and clarify together. Our approach is **start with a conversation, not a punishment.** We see first incidents as teachable moments.

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Teacher–student conversation** | The teacher asks you to explain your thinking, show draft history, or describe how AI was used. If misuse was a misunderstanding, the teacher clarifies expectations. First incidents are treated as learning opportunities. |
| **2. Demonstrate understanding** | If the conversation is inconclusive, you can demonstrate mastery through an oral defense, an in-class writing prompt on the same topic, or by presenting your full prompt history. |
| **3. Administrative review** | If a student knowingly bypassed the learning process, families are contacted. The situation follows the Student Code of Conduct. |
| **4. High-stakes situations** | In cases affecting grades, eligibility, or graduation, the district maintains a high standard of evidence and due process. |

## 9. Questions we are still working through

**Honesty about what we don't know yet is a sign of a responsible community.** We don't pretend to have all the answers. Here are some things the community is actively exploring:

- **Passing vs. learning.** When grades feel like the only goal, AI becomes a shortcut. How do we shift classroom culture so the process of learning is more rewarding than a fast answer?
- **Where is the "polishing" line?** Generating a whole essay is cheating. But how much AI editing is too much? We are still defining that boundary together.
- **AI detectors and fairness.** How do we protect students who are falsely flagged while still having meaningful integrity conversations?
- **Long-term critical thinking.** How will we track whether foundational research and memory skills are holding up over time?
- **Equitable access.** If some students have paid, high-powered AI tools at home and others do not, how do we ensure AI doesn't widen the achievement gap? This is a district-wide equity commitment.

**Student voices shape what comes next.** We will find answers together through student focus groups, Teacher-Based Team (TBT) feedback, and parent listening sessions.

## A starter-hermes compass

Use this profile with the same care you use in class.

- **Process first.** Show your thinking. AI is your tutor, not your ghostwriter.
- **Cite it.** Name the tool, describe the use, show how you verified it.
- **Protect your privacy.** No passwords, no API keys, no personal information in prompts.
- **Verify.** When the AI says something important, check it.
- **Talk it through.** If something feels off, ask your teacher. Conversation before consequence.
